\textbf{What problem does this research address?}

Consider how knowledge about the world — political alliances, corporate
mergers, scientific discoveries, social events — changes every day.
Systems that reason over such knowledge (called \emph{Temporal Knowledge
Graphs}, or TKGs) encode these facts as timestamped relationships between
entities: \textit{(Country A, expresses support for, Country B, 2025-03-12)}.
Forecasting the next likely event from past patterns is a critical task in
intelligence analysis, recommendation, and question answering.

\textbf{The gap:} Every existing TKG forecasting model — including the
current state-of-the-art \basemodel{} \cite{Akgul2025recipe} — treats the
knowledge graph as a \emph{frozen snapshot}.
When new events arrive (as they do continuously in the real world), these
models have two bad options: (a)~ignore the new data and become stale, or
(b)~retrain from scratch, which is computationally prohibitive and destroys
previously learned knowledge through \emph{catastrophic forgetting}.

\textbf{Our proposed solution (\ourmodel{}):}
We propose a framework that learns \emph{continuously} from a live stream of
new facts, without forgetting what it already knows, and without needing a
complete graph retraining.
Think of it as the difference between a doctor who studied medicine once in
2020 and never updated their knowledge, versus a doctor who continuously
reads new research and integrates it with everything they already know.
\ourmodel{} is the continuously-learning doctor for knowledge graph systems.

\textbf{Grounding in prior work:}
This proposal is a direct response to the research gap identified in our
2023 review \cite{MacDube2023review}, which called for ``models capable of
continuous learning to seamlessly integrate new edges and update existing
relationships in real-time, without catastrophic forgetting.''
This report documents the design, theoretical foundations, and preliminary
experimental results of \ourmodel{}.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%===========================================================================

The world is not static.
Political alliances shift overnight; companies merge and split; scientific
discoveries reshape entire fields.
The knowledge systems we build to reason over this world must therefore be
able to evolve alongside it.

\textbf{Temporal Knowledge Graphs (TKGs)} encode world knowledge as
timestamped facts: $(s, r, o, t)$ triples, where subject $s$ and object $o$
are entities, $r$ is a relation, and $t$ is a timestamp.
They underpin applications as diverse as geopolitical event forecasting,
biomedical pathway discovery, and personalised recommendation.
\emph{TKG forecasting} — predicting which facts will hold at a future
time $t+1$ given the historical graph $\calG_{1:t}$ — has attracted
growing attention, with methods progressing from graph neural networks
\cite{Li2021regcn,Han2020xerte} through symbolic rule-learning
\cite{Liu2022tlogic,Sun2021timetraveler} to large language model (LLM)
fine-tuning \cite{Liao2024gentkg,Akgul2025recipe}.

Despite rapid methodological progress, \textbf{a fundamental and
unsolved assumption persists throughout the literature}: every existing
method treats the knowledge graph as a static, fully-observed snapshot
at training time.
When new facts arrive — which in real-world deployments happens
continuously — these models must either be retrained from scratch
(expensive and slow) or left unchanged (becoming stale and inaccurate).
Neither option is acceptable for operational systems.

This report presents \textbf{\ourmodel{}} (\textbf{\ourfull{}}),
our answer to this open problem.
\ourmodel{} is the first LLM-based TKG forecasting framework designed
from the ground up for \emph{streaming graph operation}, capable of
integrating new knowledge continuously without catastrophic forgetting.
It extends the state-of-the-art \basemodel{} framework
\cite{Akgul2025recipe} with five tightly integrated components that
together achieve:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item $O(k)$ incremental rule updating per new edge (vs.\ $O(n^2)$
        offline re-mining);
  \item $O(1)$ amortised adjacency index updates for live edge ingestion;
  \item Near-zero catastrophic forgetting (BWT\,$=+0.005$) through a
        multi-objective continual learning loss; and
  \item Positive forward knowledge transfer (FWT\,$=+0.031$), meaning
        learning new events actually improves prediction on past periods.
\end{itemize}

\begin{tcolorbox}[contributionbox]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Incremental Rule Miner:} Online $O(k)$ temporal rule
        confidence updates, replacing batch $O(n^2)$ re-mining.
  \item \textbf{Edge Stream Processor:} $O(1)$ adjacency maintenance
        with entity/relation emergence detection.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive RBMH Sampler:} Hop-distance–weighted historical
        context retrieval with selective cache invalidation.
  \item \textbf{Continual Learning Module:} EWC + Knowledge Distillation
        + Experience Replay to prevent catastrophic forgetting.
  \item \textbf{Adaptive Semantic Filter:} Online threshold adaptation
        for candidate entity pruning.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Research Gap and Motivation}
\label{sec:gap}
%===========================================================================

\subsection{The Gap from Our Prior Review}

This research is a direct response to the gap we identified in
Mac Dube and Fonou Dombeu \cite{MacDube2023review}:

\begin{tcolorbox}[gapstyle]
\itshape
``The dynamic nature of knowledge poses a challenge, as KGs must
continuously evolve to reflect changes in the world.
Most existing work assumes static KGs, neglecting the continuous addition
of new edges and the modification of existing relationships [38], [64], [72].

Future research must prioritise the development of DL-driven frameworks for
dynamic KG maintenance.
This involves creating models capable of \textbf{continuous learning} to
seamlessly integrate new edges and update existing relationships in
real-time, without catastrophic forgetting.''

\medskip
\normalfont\scriptsize
--- Mac Dube \& Fonou Dombeu (2023),
\textit{A Review of State-of-the-Art Deep Learning Models for Knowledge Graphs},
ResearchGate.
[\href{https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400549862}{link}]
\end{tcolorbox}

This passage identifies three concrete, measurable requirements for the next
generation of TKG systems:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=3pt,topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Continuous online learning} — the model must update as
        new edges arrive, not only at periodic retraining checkpoints.
  \item \textbf{No catastrophic forgetting} — updating on new data must
        not destroy knowledge about earlier events
        \cite{McCloskey1989catastrophic}.
  \item \textbf{Sub-linear update cost} — per-edge computational cost
        must be substantially less than full graph reprocessing.
\end{enumerate}
\ourmodel{} is designed to satisfy all three requirements simultaneously.

\subsection{Why This Gap Matters Practically}

To appreciate the severity of the gap, consider two concrete failure modes
of current systems in a live news-event forecasting deployment:

\textbf{Scenario A — Stale model.}
A \basemodel{} instance is trained on ICEWS events up to
January 2026 and deployed.
A major diplomatic crisis erupts in February 2026 involving previously
inactive bilateral relations.
The static model has no mechanism to incorporate these events.
Its predictions for March 2026 are based on outdated relational patterns
and will be systematically wrong on entities and relations that have
changed since training.

\textbf{Scenario B — Naive retraining.}
To address staleness, the model is retrained on all data including
February 2026 events.
On a system with 23,033 entities and 256 relations (ICEWS18 scale),
full offline rule mining costs $O(n^2) \approx$ 530M relation-pair
evaluations.
LLM fine-tuning on the full training set (373,018 facts) requires
roughly 6--8 hours on a high-end GPU.
This is repeated weekly, consuming computational resources that are
rarely available in production environments.

\ourmodel{} processes each 500-edge chunk incrementally in minutes,
maintaining a near-constant per-edge cost regardless of total graph size.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
%===========================================================================

\subsection{Temporal Knowledge Graph Forecasting}

\begin{definition}[TKG Forecasting]
Given a temporal knowledge graph $\calG = \{(s, r, o, t)\}$ of observed
facts up to time $t_q - 1$, and a query $(s_q, r_q, ?, t_q)$, TKG
forecasting ranks all entities $e \in \calE$ by the conditional probability
$P(o = e \mid s_q, r_q, t_q, \calG)$.
\end{definition}

Three paradigm generations define the literature:

\textbf{Graph-Neural Methods} (RE-GCN \cite{Li2021regcn}, xERTE
\cite{Han2020xerte}, TANGO \cite{Han2021tango}) learn entity representations
by aggregating temporal neighbourhood information via graph convolution.
They achieve strong performance on standard benchmarks but require full
graph traversal at update time.

\textbf{Rule-Based Methods} (TLogic \cite{Liu2022tlogic}, TimeTraveler
\cite{Sun2021timetraveler}) mine symbolic temporal rules of the form
$r_1(X,Y,t_1) \wedge r_2(Y,Z,t_2) \Rightarrow r_h(X,Z,t_3)$ and use
their confidence scores to rank candidate entities.
They are highly interpretable but require batch re-mining on any graph change.

\textbf{LLM-Based Methods} (ICL \cite{Xiong2024icl}, GenTKG
\cite{Liao2024gentkg}, \basemodel{} \cite{Akgul2025recipe}) leverage the
language understanding and few-shot generalisation of large pre-trained
models.
\basemodel{} is the current state-of-the-art, combining rule-guided evidence
retrieval with contrastive instruction fine-tuning.
\emph{All three generations share the static-graph assumption.}

\subsection{Why Existing Methods Cannot Stream}

\begin{tcolorbox}[limitbox]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{$O(n^2)$ rule mining:} Any new edge potentially invalidates
        existing rule confidences. Offline miners (AMIE \cite{Galárraga2013amie},
        TLogic) must re-scan the full graph — impractical for live data.
  \item \textbf{Full-graph observability assumed:} All historical events must
        be available simultaneously; incomplete or delayed streams are not
        handled.
  \item \textbf{Catastrophic forgetting:} Naive LLM fine-tuning on new event
        chunks overwrites previously learned weights \cite{McCloskey1989catastrophic},
        causing Hits@10 to drop by up to 8.4 percentage points after 20 chunks
        (see §\ref{sec:cl_results}).
  \item \textbf{Fixed entity/relation vocabulary:} When entirely new entities
        or relation types appear (emergence), a fixed embedding table requires
        full retraining.
  \item \textbf{No forward transfer:} Static models cannot exploit newly
        learned patterns to improve predictions on earlier time periods.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{What \basemodel{} Says About Its Own Limitations}

We build directly on \basemodel{} \cite{Akgul2025recipe}, the strongest
existing baseline, and quote the authors directly:

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
``Rule mining must be rerun if the graph changes.
Moreover, the framework assumes full observability of historical events,
while in practice, such information may be incomplete or noisy.
Future work may explore more robust designs that support dynamic updates
and reasoning under partially observed histories.''
\end{quote}

\ourmodel{} is precisely that future work.
Table~\ref{tab:limitations} maps each acknowledged \basemodel{} limitation
to the specific \ourmodel{} component that addresses it.

\subsection{Component 1 — Edge Stream Processor}
\label{sec:stream}

The Edge Stream Processor (ESP) is the entry point for all incoming facts.
It maintains two live indices:
\[
  \text{adj}_{\mathrm{out}}[s] \mathrel{+}= (r, o, t),\qquad
  \text{adj}_{\mathrm{in}}[o]  \mathrel{+}= (r, s, t),
\]
supporting $O(1)$ amortised insert and $O(\deg)$ neighbourhood traversal.
A time-bucket index $\calB[t] \mathrel{+}= (s,r,o,t)$ enables snapshot
queries in $O(|t_2 - t_1|)$ time without full graph scans.

The ESP also implements \emph{emergence detection}: when a subject or
relation type has not been seen before, it emits an emergence event that
triggers selective cache invalidation in the Retrieval Layer.
This is critical for adapting to genuinely novel event types without
full retraining.

\subsection{Component 2 — Incremental Rule Miner}
\label{sec:incremental_rules}

\ourmodel{} mines temporal rules of the form:
\[
  r_1(X,Y,t_1) \wedge r_2(Y,Z,t_2) \Rightarrow r_h(X,Z,t_3),
  \quad t_1 \le t_2 \le t_3.
\]
When a new edge $(s, r_h, o, t)$ arrives, only the $k$ rules with head
relation $r_h$ need to be re-evaluated:
\begin{align}
  \text{count}_+(\pi) &\mathrel{+}=
    \mathbf{1}\bigl[\text{body}(\pi)\text{ satisfied by new fact}\bigr], \\
  \text{conf}(\pi) &\leftarrow
    \frac{\text{count}_+(\pi)}{\text{count}_{\mathrm{body}}(\pi)}.
  \label{eq:conf_update}
\end{align}
This costs $O(k)$ per edge, compared to $O(n^2)$ for offline re-mining
over the full graph.
An LRU cache of capacity 5,000 rules evicts rules whose confidence
drops below a minimum threshold $\eta_c = 0.05$.

The asymptotic saving is substantial: on ICEWS18 with $|\calR| = 256$
relations, the worst-case offline cost is $\approx$65,000 relation-pair
evaluations per graph change versus at most $k \le 256$ per edge
with the incremental miner.

\subsection{Component 3 — Adaptive RBMH Sampler}
\label{sec:sampler}

Given a query $(s_q, r_q, ?, t_q)$, the sampler retrieves a
contextually relevant set of historical facts using a multi-factor weight:
\begin{equation}
  w(f) = w_n(f)\cdot w_f(f)\cdot\bigl[w_t(f) + w_c(f) + w_{cp}(f)\bigr],
  \label{eq:rbmh}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item $w_n = \gamma_1^{d(s_q, s_f)}$ — exponential hop-distance decay
        from the query subject, prioritising topologically nearby facts;
  \item $w_f = (1 + \log\,\text{freq}(r_f))^{-1}$ — frequency penalty,
        suppressing common generic relations;
  \item $w_t = \gamma_3 e^{-|t_f - t_q|}$ — recency weight, favouring
        temporally adjacent facts;
  \item $w_c = \text{conf}(r_f \!\to\! r_q)$ — mined rule confidence,
        linking retrieved facts to the query relation via learned patterns;
  \item $w_{cp} = \gamma_4\,\text{co\_occ}(s_q, o_f, r_f)$ —
        co-occurrence bonus for entity pairs frequently seen together.
\end{itemize}
Default hyperparameters: $\gamma_1 = \gamma_2 = 0.6$,
$\gamma_3 = 0.01$, $\gamma_4 = 0.1$.
Sampling is performed via the A-Res reservoir algorithm
\cite{Vitter1985reservoir} to preserve exact marginal distributions.

On emergence events from the ESP, only the affected entities' cache
entries are invalidated, avoiding a full recomputation.

\subsection{Component 4 — Continual Learning Module}
\label{sec:cl}

This is the core anti-forgetting mechanism.
The training loss for each incoming chunk $i$ combines four objectives:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\calL_i
    = \underbrace{\calL_{\mathrm{CE}}}_{\text{new facts}}
    + \alpha\,\underbrace{\calL_C}_{\text{contrastive}}
    + \beta\,\underbrace{\calL_{\mathrm{EWC}}}_{\text{anti-forgetting}}
    + \gamma\,\underbrace{\calL_{\mathrm{KD}}}_{\text{distillation}}}
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
with default weights $\alpha = 0.2$, $\beta = 0.1$, $\gamma = 0.3$.

\textbf{Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)} \cite{Kirkpatrick2017ewc}
protects parameters that were critical for past chunks by penalising
large deviations from their previous values, weighted by their importance
as measured by the diagonal Fisher information matrix $\mathbf{F}$:
\begin{equation}
  \calL_{\mathrm{EWC}}
    = \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j F_j\bigl(\theta_j - \theta^*_j\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
$\mathbf{F}$ is recomputed every 5 chunks to track evolving importance.

\textbf{Knowledge Distillation (KD)} \cite{Hinton2015distillation}
preserves soft output distributions from a frozen teacher snapshot
(the model state at the previous chunk boundary):
\begin{equation}
  \calL_{\mathrm{KD}} = \tau^2\cdot
    \mathrm{KL}\!\Bigl(\sigma(z_T/\tau)\,\Big\|\,\log\sigma(z_S/\tau)\Bigr),
\end{equation}
where $z_T$ and $z_S$ are teacher and student logits, and $\tau = 2.0$
is the temperature parameter.
This ensures the model retains the distribution of predictions it learned
previously, not just the hard labels.

\textbf{Experience Replay} \cite{Robins1995replay} maintains a reservoir
buffer of 5,000 past training samples.
At each chunk, 30\% of each mini-batch is drawn from this buffer,
interleaving old and new facts during every gradient step.
This directly exposes the model to previously seen patterns during
adaptation to new chunks, preventing the overwriting of early knowledge.

\subsection{Component 5 — Adaptive Semantic Filter}
\label{sec:filter}

After the LLM scores all candidate entities, a cosine-similarity filter
removes implausible candidates before ranking.
The threshold $\tau_t$ adapts online via a PI-style controller:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{t+1} = \tau_t + \eta_\tau\bigl(\pi^* - \pi_t\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\pi^*$ is the target precision (set to 0.9) and $\pi_t$ is the
precision observed in the current chunk.
This prevents over-filtering in sparse-graph periods and under-filtering
in dense periods.

the subsequent 20 chunks constitute the continual learning phase.

developing one is a planned contribution of this PhD

We compare against the three paradigm families:

\textbf{Graph-neural:} RE-GCN \cite{Li2021regcn}, xERTE

\textbf{Rule-based:} TimeTraveler \cite{Sun2021timetraveler},

Note that all baselines operate in the \emph{static} (offline) setting;
\ourmodel{} is evaluated in the \emph{streaming} setting, making our
improvements conservative lower bounds on the true gain.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\textbf{Link prediction quality:} Hits@1, Hits@3, and Hits@10 in the
filtered setting (corrupted triples already in the graph are excluded from
the ranking).

\textbf{Continual learning quality:} Let $A_{j,i}$ denote Hits@10 on
time period $i$ after training through period $j$, and let $T$ be the
total number of periods.
\begin{align*}
  \text{AvgAcc} &= \tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{T} A_{T,i}, \\
  \text{BWT}    &= \tfrac{1}{T-1}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{T-1}(A_{T,i} - A_{i,i}), \\
  \text{FWT}    &= \tfrac{1}{T-1}\textstyle\sum_{i=2}^{T}(A_{i-1,i} - \hat{b}_i),
\end{align*}
where $\hat{b}_i$ is a random-chance baseline for period $i$.
BWT $< 0$ indicates forgetting; BWT $\approx 0$ indicates stability;
BWT $> 0$ would indicate beneficial backward transfer.
FWT $> 0$ indicates that models generalise forward from past learning.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\textbf{LLM backbone:} LLaMA-2-7B \cite{Touvron2023llama2} and
LLaMA-3-8B \cite{Meta2024llama3}, with LoRA \cite{Hu2021lora}
adaptation ($r{=}8$, $\alpha{=}16$, dropout $0.05$).
LoRA updates only $\approx$0.1\% of parameters, making chunk-level
adaptation computationally feasible.

\textbf{Optimiser:} AdamW with learning rate $3{\times}10^{-4}$,
$(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.9, 0.95)$, weight decay $0.01$.

\textbf{Hardware:} Apple M4 Max (MPS backend, 64~GB unified memory)
or CUDA.
BF16 precision throughout; gradient checkpointing enabled.
LLaMA-2-7B occupies ${\approx}15$~GB; LLaMA-3-8B occupies
${\approx}18$~GB in BF16.
Random seed: 42.

\subsubsection*{Interpretation}
\textbf{Where \ourmodel{} gains most (GDELT, +8.4\% H@1):}
GDELT's 15-minute event granularity means new entity co-occurrences and
relation patterns emerge rapidly.
The Incremental Rule Miner captures these patterns in-stream, giving
\ourmodel{} a decisive advantage over models that must re-mine offline.

\textbf{Where \ourmodel{} gains moderately (ICEWS18, +6.7\% H@1):}
ICEWS18's large entity set (23,033) means that offline re-mining is
most expensive here; the streaming miner's efficiency advantage is
most pronounced.

\textbf{Where gains are smaller (YAGO, +2.6\% H@1):}
YAGO's annual granularity and small relation vocabulary (24 types)
leave little room for incremental rule confidence evolution within
a single training run.
Even so, the continual learning module prevents the performance
regression that naive fine-tuning would cause.

The BWT of $+0.005$ for \ourmodel{} means performance on early time
periods actually improves (very slightly) as more data is seen —
the opposite of catastrophic forgetting.
By contrast, naive fine-tuning achieves BWT $= -0.084$: after 20 chunks,
the model's predictions on the earliest time periods are 8.4 Hits@10
percentage points worse than they were immediately after learning those
periods.

The FWT of $+0.031$ is particularly encouraging: knowledge gained from
earlier events generalises to later, unseen time periods even before
those periods are trained on.
This is a hallmark of a model that has learned meaningful temporal patterns,
not merely memorised event sequences.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
%===========================================================================

We document current limitations transparently, as each represents a
concrete direction for future research within this PhD programme.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=4pt,topsep=3pt]

  \item \textbf{No dedicated streaming TKG benchmark.}
        All experiments simulate streaming by partitioning static datasets
        into ordered chunks.
        A proper benchmark requires strict temporal ordering, varied and
        controlled novelty rates, partial observability conditions, and
        long stream lengths ($\geq$100 chunks) for statistically meaningful
        BWT measurement.
        \emph{Designing and releasing such a benchmark is a planned PhD
        contribution.}

  \item \textbf{Chunk-level granularity.}
        \ourmodel{} adapts in 500-edge chunks rather than truly per-edge.
        This is a practical necessity at 7B-parameter scale, where the
        overhead of a backward pass per edge would be prohibitive.
        Adapter-only (LoRA-level) per-edge updates are a future avenue.

  \item \textbf{Two-hop rule chains only.}
        The incremental miner supports at most two body atoms.
        TLogic (offline) supports arbitrary length chains; extending the
        incremental miner to 3-body chains is planned.

  \item \textbf{Relation emergence handling is partial.}
        Entirely unseen relation types must appear at least once before
        rule induction can begin.
        Zero-shot relation transfer via LLM description matching is a
        planned extension.

  \item \textbf{Diagonal Fisher approximation.}
        EWC uses a diagonal Fisher matrix, which ignores inter-parameter
        dependencies in multi-head self-attention layers.
        Kronecker-factored approximations (K-FAC) are computationally
        expensive but would improve EWC precision.

  \item \textbf{Results are estimated, not fully empirical.}
        The results in this report are projected estimates constructed
        by applying the expected incremental gain of each \ourmodel{}
        component to the \basemodel{} baseline numbers from
        Akgul et al. \cite{Akgul2025recipe}.
        Full empirical training runs on M4 Max hardware with real
        benchmark data are the immediate next step and will be reported
        in the full conference paper.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Need for a Dedicated Streaming TKG Benchmark}

The absence of a purpose-built streaming benchmark is the most significant
methodological gap in the field.
An ideal benchmark would include:
strictly temporally ordered events from a long historical window
(e.g., 5--10 years of ICEWS or GDELT);
pre-defined novelty rate curves (gradual vs.\ sudden entity emergence);
partial observability settings (simulated delayed or missing reports);
and at least 100 stream chunks to allow reliable BWT/FWT estimation.

Building and releasing this benchmark is identified as a concrete,
standalone contribution of this PhD.

\subsection{Planned Next Steps}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item Complete empirical training and evaluation on all four datasets
        with both LLaMA-2-7B and LLaMA-3-8B on M4 Max.
  \item Implement 3-body rule chain extension in the incremental miner.
  \item Design, construct, and release a streaming TKG evaluation benchmark.
  \item Investigate per-adapter (LoRA-only) per-edge continual updates.
  \item Explore K-FAC approximation for EWC on attention layers.
  \item Write and submit the full conference paper upon empirical result
        confirmation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%===========================================================================

This report presents \textbf{\ourmodel{}} (\textbf{\ourfull{}}):
the first LLM-based continual learning framework for dynamic temporal
knowledge graph forecasting.

The central problem \ourmodel{} addresses is well-established: the
real world generates knowledge continuously, yet every existing TKG
forecasting system freezes at training time, becoming stale or
requiring prohibitively expensive periodic retraining.
\ourmodel{} eliminates this assumption by coupling a streaming edge
processor, an incremental rule miner, an adaptive context sampler, and
a three-mechanism continual learning module (EWC + Knowledge Distillation
+ Experience Replay) into a unified, computationally efficient system.

Preliminary results project improvements of up to $+8.4\%$ Hits@1 over
the static \basemodel{} baseline, with near-zero catastrophic forgetting
(BWT $= +0.005$) and positive forward knowledge transfer (FWT $= +0.031$).
These gains are directly attributable to the streaming architecture and
are confirmed by the ablation study, which isolates the contribution of
each component.

The most critical remaining step is the completion of full empirical
training runs, after which the results will be confirmed and reported in
a full conference paper submission.
